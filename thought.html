Interactive entertainment thought leadership

Four ways to make interactive entertainment with AI, from code a model writes to pictures a model dreams. Each trades control for speed differently.

Read left to right: the left keeps rules, state and physics exact and the look simple; the right gives up control for a look no small team could render. Kindling builds in the second column and borrows from the third and fourth.

Code-first: exact rules, simple lookModel-first: rich look, loose rules

Vibe-coded simple games, bespoke engines

You vibe a whole simple game with a few prompts: the AI writes the game and its own small engine, you play it, ask for changes, and never read the code. One person, an evening, a link to share.

Good at

  • Hours from idea to a playable, shareable link.
  • Clear mechanics: arcade, puzzle, platformer, small multiplayer.
  • Fearless iteration; the engine is disposable.

Breaks at

  • Content scale: art, animation and levels still need a pipeline.
  • Long-lived code nobody understands.
  • Console, mobile store and live-ops requirements.

State of the art, 2026

  • A widely read developer called October 2026 an inflection point: current models build fun browser games with little guidance.
  • Browser game jams and prompt-to-game platforms now count creators in the tens of thousands.

Available now

  • Rosebud AI browser prompt-to-game platform; about 70,000 creators and over a million games.
  • Nilo browser 3D game creation in open beta, on its own WebGPU engine.
  • VibeGame open-source declarative engine on three.js, built for AI-assisted game making.
  • Coding agents plus three.js the route behind most viral vibe-coded games.

To watch

  • Browser game jams with prizes and no-download rules, the proving ground for the format.
  • Multiplayer vibe-coded relay servers now give small browser games 2 to 4 players.

Complex games on commercial engines

Agents build a real game in Unity or Unreal: scenes, assets, rigs, builds and tests, through the engine's own tools and an asset pipeline with standards and judges.

Good at

  • Exact control: physics, state, saves, networking, performance per platform.
  • Shipping: stores, consoles, mobile tiers, patches.
  • Reproducible builds a team can review and revert.

Breaks at

  • Asset cost: AAA characters, worlds and animation are still the bottleneck.
  • Pipeline glue between tools, where most failures happen.
  • The look is only as good as the art that goes in.

State of the art, 2026

  • Agents can now drive the editor and batch builds; the frontier is the asset pipeline, not the code.
  • Concept to production sheet to mesh to rig is partly automated; animation and world-building are the open problems.

Available now

  • Unity AI (open beta) in-editor Assistant with Ask, Plan and Agent modes; an official MCP server so outside agents drive the editor; an AI Gateway for your own models; Generators for placeholder assets.
  • Blender MCP Blender driven by agents; our greybox and animation route.

To watch

  • Unity Generators beyond placeholders today they make dev-stage assets tagged as AI-generated, to be replaced before shipping.
  • Production-quality image-to-3D our E2 bake-off decides what is usable for hero meshes.

Interactive films: simple geometry, style transfer

A simple, cheap world (greybox geometry, basic lighting, a few rules) is rendered by an engine, and a neural pass restyles every frame into a rich, consistent look.

Good at

  • A rich look without building rich assets.
  • Keeps the engine's control: geometry, collisions and state stay exact.
  • One world, many looks: swap the style, not the art.

Breaks at

  • Frame-to-frame flicker and drift over long play.
  • Speed: high quality is still far from real time on consumer hardware.
  • Small details players need to read (ledges, items) can be restyled away.

State of the art, 2026

  • Anorak (London and Chengdu, founded by the team behind Hutch) is the company to watch: its medium is interactive generative video, video that reacts to the player in real time, built on its own inference technology to make it faster and cheaper. It raised $5M in November 2026 with Supercell and MTG in the round, and plans playable formats and a creator platform for early 2027.
  • Research now enhances engine frames towards photoreal in real time by distilling a heavy offline model into a light one.
  • Style transfer that reads the engine's depth and buffers is markedly steadier than plain video stylisation.
  • Fully generated, playable world models stream minutes of interactive 720p video from a prompt.

Available now

  • Odyssey-2 Pro world model API streaming interactive 720p video at about 22 fps.
  • World Labs Marble image, video or text to a navigable 3D world in minutes, by API.
  • Decart Mirage and Lucy real-time video-to-video: restyles a live feed or a game as it plays.
  • Project Genie (Google) Genie 3 worlds for Google AI Ultra subscribers in the US; 60-second sessions at 720p.

To watch

  • Anorak playable generative video formats and a creator platform planned for early 2027.
  • Decart Oasis 3 hours-long real-time world model, by API; built for physical AI, a sign of where games go.
  • In-engine style transfer research real-time photoreal enhancement and depth-aware style transfer for engine frames.
Kindling's usePrevis and look tests: greybox from Blender, restyled to the film frames, judged before any art is built. Where Anorak generates the whole picture, we keep the geometry and rules in the engine and generate only the look.

Generated films from complex game content

The game's own characters, places, keyframes and story become the locked references for a video model, which generates trailers, cinematics and episodes at film quality.

Good at

  • Film-quality pictures from game assets a small team already owns.
  • Marketing and story: trailers, intros, episode recaps.
  • Motion reference: generated clips can be rotoscoped back into the game.

Breaks at

  • Consistency of faces, props and places across shots.
  • Audience scepticism: obvious AI artefacts draw ridicule.
  • Rights and credits for every reference that goes in.

State of the art, 2026

  • The working method is asset-first: lock characters and places once, script shot by shot, then generate each shot from the references.
  • Full-length generated features exist; critics still find them unconvincing.

Available now

  • Google Veo 3.1 strongest for cinematic shots with generated sound.
  • Kling 3.0 connected cinematic shots, strong value.
  • Runway Gen-4.5 and Aleph shot control and editing for professionals.
  • Seedance 2.0 and 2.5 reference-driven: images, motion and audio as inputs, multi-shot storyboards.

To watch

  • Sora retired: OpenAI shut its API on 24 September 2026, a reminder to keep a second model on every pipeline.
  • Open models (Wan) self-hosted video generation at zero licence cost, for a local GPU.
  • Challengers new entrants such as Alibaba's HappyHorse-1.0.
Kindling's useThe trailer keyframe work and E9: live-action stills and clips from our canon, then back into the game as motion.

Our position

  1. The columns converge. The winning team builds exact games in column two, previews them through column three, and sells and animates them through column four.
  2. Control is the moat. Players forgive a plain look; they do not forgive a ledge that moves. Keep rules, collisions and state in the engine; let models own the pixels only where nothing depends on them.
  3. References beat prompts. Every column gets better when a locked canon (characters, places, palette, motion guides) drives the model, not free text.
  4. The bottleneck is assets, not code. Code is nearly free now; characters, animation and worlds at quality are where a 10 to 20 person team wins or loses.
